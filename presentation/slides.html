Problem Statement & Motivation

Why Standard RL Fails in the Real World

In standard Reinforcement Learning, an agent is trained and tested in the exact same Markov Decision Process (MDP):

$$M_{\mathrm{train}} = (\mathcal{S}, \mathcal{A}, P_{\mathrm{train}}, R, \gamma)$$

When deployed on a physical system ($M_{\mathrm{test}} \neq M_{\mathrm{train}}$), three foundational assumptions break:

  1. Imperfect / Attacked Sensors: Observations are corrupted by random hardware noise or targeted adversarial attacks ($\tilde s_t \neq s_t$).
  2. Actuation Latency: Motor commands experience communication or mechanical delay ($\tilde a_t = a_{t-k}$).
  3. Shifted Physics (Sim-to-Real Gap): Payload mass, limb inertia, and ground friction differ from training ($P_{\mathrm{test}} \neq P_{\mathrm{train}}$).

Core Research Questions

  • RQ1 (Mid-Sem Focus — Diagnosis & Iso-Fragility Calibration): When we take a clean-trained RL policy and gradually increase disturbance intensity across different channels, which shift breaks the agent fastest, and how do we calibrate equal-difficulty breaking points ($\epsilon_{50}$) across incompatible physical units?
  • RQ2 & RQ3 (End-Sem Focus — Cross-Channel Transfer & Unified Defense): Does a robustness strategy trained for one disturbance channel transfer positively or negatively to unseen channels, and can we train a unified multi-channel defense?

Environments & Baseline Algorithm (SAC)

Two Contrasting Continuous-Control Benchmarks (MuJoCo)

Both environments share the exact same dimensions (17-D continuous state space $\mathbb{R}^{17}$, 6-D continuous action space $[-1, 1]^6$), isolating the role of mechanical stability:

Environment State $\mathcal{S}$ Action $\mathcal{A}$ Control Step $\Delta t$ Mechanical Stability & Failure Mode
Walker2d-v5 (Bipedal Walker) $\mathbb{R}^{17}$ $[-1, 1]^6$ 8 ms Unstable: Must actively balance its torso; terminates early if it falls.
HalfCheetah-v5 (Planar Runner) $\mathbb{R}^{17}$ $[-1, 1]^6$ 50 ms Mechanically Stable: Cannot fall (always runs 1,000 steps); tests gait coordination.
  • What is inside the 17-D State Vector $s_t \in \mathbb{R}^{17}$?
    • 8 positions/angles (torso height $z$, torso pitch angle, and 6 joint angles: hip, knee, and ankle/foot on both legs) + 9 velocities ($x$-velocity, $z$-velocity, torso angular velocity, and 6 joint angular velocities) = 17 continuous numbers.
  • What is inside the 6-D Action Vector $a_t \in [-1, 1]^6$?
    • 6 continuous motor torques (each normalized between $-1.0$ and $+1.0$) applied to the 6 leg joints at every control step.

Why Soft Actor-Critic (SAC) Instead of Tabular TD or Monte Carlo?

  • Continuous Spaces ($\mathbb{R}^{17} \times [-1,1]^6$): Tabular Q-learning cannot store infinite states, and discrete greedy maximization $\arg\max_a Q(s,a)$ cannot search over infinite continuous torques.
  • Why Not Monte Carlo? 1,000-step episodes create high return variance, and on-policy updates discard past data.
  • How SAC Solves Both:
    1. Twin Critics $Q_{\phi_1}(s,a), Q_{\phi_2}(s,a)$ (1-Step Off-Policy TD Learning): Reuses a $10^6$-transition Replay Buffer with low variance; takes $\min(Q_1, Q_2)$ to prevent overestimation bias.
    2. Actor Network $\pi_\theta(s)$ (Continuous Greedy + Entropy Exploration): Outputs continuous action maximizing $Q(s,a)$ plus an Entropy bonus $\alpha \mathcal{H}(\pi(\cdot \mid s))$ during training. At test time, we freeze $\pi_\theta(s)$ and use the deterministic greedy mean action.

Deep Dive — Random Noise vs. Adversarial Attack & Failure Mechanics

1. Random Gaussian Noise vs. White-Box PGD Critic Attack

Normalized return vs. sensor perturbation magnitude (log scale): Random Gaussian Noise (solid) vs. 10-step PGD Attack (dashed).
  • Why is the PGD Attack 2.4× to 3.2× Stronger at the Exact Same Budget?
    • Random noise adds an isotropic vector in $\mathbb{R}^{17}$—most directions are orthogonal to the network's decision boundary and cancel out.
    • Our 10-step PGD attack uses $\nabla_\delta Q(s_t, \pi_\theta(s_t + \delta))$ to push the observation along the steepest descent direction of the Critic, tricking the Actor into selecting the worst possible torque for the current state $s_t$.
    • On HalfCheetah-v5, an adversarial nudge of just 4.4% of 1 standard deviation ($\epsilon_{80} = 0.044$) already wipes out 20% of total return!

2. How Do the Robots Actually Fail? (Falls & Policy Sensitivity)

Left & Middle: Fall rate of bipedal Walker2d-v5. Right: Policy action deviation $\lVert\pi_\theta(\tilde s) - \pi_\theta(s)\rVert_2$.
  • Left & Middle Panels (Bipedal Falls in Walker2d-v5):
    • Clean Walker2d-v5 falls in only 6.7% of episodes.
    • Under adversarial sensor attack ($\epsilon = 0.2$), falls surge to 77% (and 100% at $\epsilon = 0.3$).
    • Under action delay, 2 steps of delay causes a 100% fall rate.
  • Right Panel — Policy Action Deviation ($\lVert\pi_\theta(\tilde s) - \pi_\theta(s)\rVert_2$):
    • Measures how much the corrupted sensor reading changes the Actor's motor command.
    • At every perturbation size $\epsilon$, the adversarial attack induces nearly 2× larger action changes than random noise, proving it exploits high-sensitivity (large local Lipschitz) directions of the policy network.

Key Takeaways from Mid-Semester Phase

  1. Action Delay is #1 Most Damaging (Control-Frequency Paradox): Memoryless policies $\pi(s_t)$ lose the Markov property under queued latency. Even though HalfCheetah-v5 cannot fall, it fails faster per step ($\epsilon_{50}=0.72$) than bipedal Walker2d-v5 ($\epsilon_{50}=1.28$) because its control step is 50 ms vs. 8 ms (6.25× longer physical lag per step).
  2. Average-Case vs. Worst-Case Robustness: Surviving random Gaussian noise up to $\sigma = 0.40$ gives a false sense of security; targeted PGD attacks break the same policy at $\epsilon = 0.13$ to $0.16$.
  3. Iso-Fragility Calibration: Mapping $\epsilon_{50}$ across all 5 channels aligns incompatible physical units into a single equal-difficulty scale ($1.0\times \epsilon_{50}$ = 50% score drop), enabling fair cross-channel transfer evaluation.

Novel Hypotheses & End-Semester Roadmap (From Diagnosis to Defense)

  1. Novel Scientific Discovery — Cross-Channel Interference & Negative Transfer Matrix:
    • Smoothness vs. Reflex Hypothesis: Defending against sensor attacks via Lipschitz policy smoothing ($\lVert\pi(\tilde s) - \pi(s)\rVert_2 \to 0$) may cause negative transfer on low-friction ice or heavy mass by making balance reflexes too sluggish.
    • Inertia vs. Latency Hypothesis: Test whether Domain Randomization over limb mass/inertia provides positive transfer to 1-step action delay.
  2. Novel Algorithmic Contribution — $\epsilon_{50}$-Calibrated Multi-Channel Stochastic Adversary:
    • Extending single-channel ATSA (Zhou et al., ICML 2025) and DRQ (Liang et al., ICML 2024), we normalize all perturbation channels by their empirical $\epsilon_{50}$ breaking points and train a maximum-entropy multi-channel adversary across sensor, delay, and physics channels simultaneously.
